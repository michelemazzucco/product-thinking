## Summary
Systrom and Krieger argue that a product should exist only because it solves a real problem. Instagram started by listing the top three problems with mobile photos (photos looked bad, sharing was fragmented, uploads were slow) and crushing each one. They later found Clayton Christensen's jobs-to-be-done theory and used it for every product: what is the user hiring this for, functionally, socially, and emotionally? New features came from watching how people "hack" the product (polls from teens asking questions in Stories, Indonesian shops selling via posts), and only in jobs adjacent to existing behavior. Timing matters: paddle for the wave at the right moment; technology without a problem means "you're paddling too early." They push "do the simple thing first" (a Sharpie whiteboard as the first ad system) and "humble but confident." For idea validation, this gives a clear test: name the problems, the job, the existing behavior, and why now.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Solve a problem, or don't exist:** "The only reason it should exist in the world is because it solves someone's problem." Instagram wrote down the top three problems with mobile photos and set out to "crush" each one: filters for bad photos, one-tap sharing to many networks, background upload for speed.
- **Jobs to be done (functional, social, emotional):** What do people hire the product to do? State the jobs explicitly, including social and emotional ones. Every product (Stories included) had to say clearly which problem it solves in someone's life.
- **Products can get worse at their job over time:** Feeds create self-limiting norms ("you never double Insta"). Instagram became worse at its original job (quickly sharing what you're up to). Stories recaptured that job.
- **Watch how people hack the product:** Look at real usage by segment (their internal "Fiddler" tool showed sample posts per demographic). Teens made solid-color Stories to ask questions, so they built a poll sticker. Hashtags existed before they were clickable. People linked Snapchat in bios, which pointed to Stories.
- **Build only in adjacent jobs with existing behavior:** An Indonesian shop posted items and deleted them when sold ("WhatsApp me" to pay). That showed a commerce job years before any commerce product. Companies fail when they are hired for one job but "jealous" of another, far-off job. With no existing behavior to divert, a new product "would probably flop."
- **Question the assumptions during a technology shift:** Many assumed people would never give up physical keyboards or upload from a camera via desktop. Winners question a fundamental assumption during a big technology transition.
- **Catch the wave at the right time:** Start paddling too early or too late and you look like an idiot. Instagram started right as the iPhone 4 made people consider leaving their camera at home. Technology in search of a problem means you're paddling too early.
- **Hyper-pragmatism over five-year visions:** Solve the problem that exists now with what's available now or within six months. That earns you the ticket to the next problem. Teams often underrate the opportunity cost of "pie in the sky" bets (their hardware prototypes went nowhere).
- **Do the simple thing first:** Use existing tools (Django, AWS, open source). The first ad "auction" was a calendar on a whiteboard. If nobody bought ads, a year building a perfect auction would have been wasted.
- **Product principles from day one:** Community first, simplicity, speed (make it smooth on the slowest phone), craft, unlocking creativity.
- **Scratch your own itch:** Quoting a VC they met: founders who search for a startup idea often never find one; those with an itch they believe others share do better. Instagram V1 was built for the two founders.
- **Isolate what works and cut the rest:** Burbn (a check-in app) was "on the bad side." They kept the parts that worked and pivoted to photos only.
- **Great and terrible ideas both look bad at first:** Everyone said photo sharing had been done. You won't get encouragement. Your job is to find out quickly which side you're on, based on conviction and data.
- **Humble but confident; "we may not be right, but we're not confused":** Be very confident in the idea but quick to learn you're wrong. Commit fully to big changes (Stories launched to almost everyone at once). Half-launches create odd social dynamics and signal you don't mean it.
- **Growth: build in the loop, then get out of the way:** Cross-posting to Twitter and Facebook linked back to Instagram; filters and borders worked as calling cards. When growth dropped, "99 out of a hundred times" it was their own bug (push notifications broke). Distribution channels depend on the era; you can't copy 2010's playbook today.
- **Monetization can wait if the path is plausible:** They pitched Benchmark with a "business model question mark" slide, betting a visual product at scale could carry visual ads.
- **Look at the raw data yourself:** The best founders inspect real examples ("half the images came in backwards"). Have a reasonable hypothesis before throwing data at a model.
- **Focus brutally early:** No external meetings for four months before launch. Then hire early once something is working, or you enter a "death spiral" of firefighting with no time to hire.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Problems and jobs**
- Write down the top three problems your product solves today. How would you "crush" each one, specifically?
- What is the customer hiring your product to do: the functional job, the social job, and the emotional job?
- Is this an itch you have yourself? If not, how do you know the problem is real and painful for others?

**Existing behavior**
- What are people already doing today, with hacks and workarounds, that your product would make easier? Show me an example.
- Is your idea adjacent to a job people already hire you (or a tool) for, or is it a job you wish they'd hire you for?
- If no one is hacking around this problem today, why do you believe they want it solved?

**Why now**
- What technology shift or wave makes this possible now? Is the wave arriving now, or are you paddling too early or too late?
- Are you a technology in search of a problem? Name the problem without mentioning the technology.
- Which "everyone knows" assumption in your market are you questioning, and what evidence says it's wrong?

**Simplicity and focus**
- What is the simplest version that tests whether anyone wants this? What are you building now that could be a manual process or a whiteboard?
- What is the one or two things you must get right before launch? What are you saying no to?

**Signal and conviction**
- Smart people say this has been done. What evidence, beyond your conviction, says you're on the "good idea that looks bad" side?
- If this doesn't work, what parts would you keep, and what would you cut? What is your Burbn-to-Instagram move?
- Is your product likely to get worse at its core job as it grows (norms, clutter, selectivity)? How would you notice?

**Growth and money**
- How does each use of the product bring in new users (a calling card, a share that links back)? Is that channel still open today, or was it a past-era tactic?
- When growth stalls, how will you know whether it's the market or something you broke?
- What is a plausible path to money at scale, and what would make it implausible?

## Red flags / failure modes
- **No named problem:** The founder can describe features and technology but not the specific problems the product solves.
- **Technology in search of a problem:** Starting from a new capability (ML, AR, etc.) with no clear job. "Paddling too early."
- **Jealous of a far-off job:** Planning a product for a job your users don't currently hire you for, with no existing behavior to build on.
- **General-purpose tool that does nothing specific well:** O'Shaughnessy's framing, which the founders answer with jobs to be done.
- **Over-building before demand:** Building the scalable system (auction engine, five-year platform) before testing if anyone wants the simple version.
- **Five-year vision over today's problem:** Ignoring the opportunity cost of "pie in the sky" bets when real users have unsolved problems now.
- **Copying an old distribution playbook:** Expecting growth channels that worked in 2010 (or three years ago) to work today.
- **Seeking validation from others:** Treating "people said it's been done" or "people said it's great" as signal, instead of data and quick tests.
