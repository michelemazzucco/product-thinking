# Daniel Ek: The Future of Audio

## Source
- **Title:** The Future of Audio (Invest Like the Best, Episode 147, November 12, 2019)
- **Speaker / author:** Daniel Ek (founder and CEO of Spotify), interviewed by Patrick O'Shaughnessy
- **URL:** https://joincolossus.com/episode/ek-the-future-of-audio/
- **Type:** transcript
- **Notes on source quality:** The current Colossus page puts the transcript behind a login. I used the full official Colossus transcript from an archived copy of the old episode page (Wayback Machine, Nov 2021 snapshot of `joincolossus.com/episodes/71376147/...?tab=transcript`). About 11,000 words, whole episode. The episode is mostly strategy at Spotify scale (2019). The parts most useful for validating a new idea are: jobs to be done, megas vs. niches, competing with free, the wedge, and "borrowing mechanics without the system."

## Summary
Ek thinks in systems. He argues that globalization, digitization, and automation polarize markets: the scale winners get far bigger, niches become viable, and the middle gets "vaporized." So you must choose to be a niche or go for scale, and most people underestimate how big "scale" now is. He defines markets by the consumer's job to be done, not by industry labels (for him, music and podcasts are one job: audio). On early Spotify, he explains how to compete with free: start from the end experience ("all the world's music on your hard drive") and reason backward, then enter through a narrow wedge (a weak Swedish market with nothing to lose) and widen it with evidence. He warns against copying a mechanic from another company without understanding the system it lives in. For idea validation, this pushes a founder to define the job, the wedge, and the scale they are really aiming for.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Megas and minnows (polarization):** Globalization, digitization, and automation make the big much bigger and make new niches viable. "The thing in the middle" gets vaporized. You must pick: a niche (can be very profitable) or true scale.
- **People underestimate scale:** Many firms think they are at scale because they were at scale in an older world. Ek's H&M example: a huge company may still address under 1% of its real market. If the job is truly global, the winner may need 20%+ of a market in the trillions.
- **Define the market by the job to be done:** Consumers have jobs (entertain me, educate me, move me from A to B). Jobs that look different can turn out to be the same once supply is consolidated. Spotify's big "point of arbitrage" was believing music and podcasts are the same job: audio. Others saw them as separate.
- **Jobs and industries get rebundled:** Taxis became "transportation," and then food delivery inside Uber became bigger than rides in some markets. The job, and who serves it, shifts.
- **Star vs. constellation:** Should you add features to one app or ship separate apps? Getting distribution for a new consumer app is very hard (people download about one new app a year). Split only when the user is a very different constituent and the job is materially different (Spotify Kids, Spotify for Artists).
- **Compete with free by starting from the end:** Piracy was free but painful (slow, viruses, wrong files, 30 to 40 minutes). Spotify started from the magic experience, "all the world's music on your hard drive," and reasoned backward. It used perception tricks (moving the progress indicator before playback) so 500 ms felt instant.
- **The wedge:** Be very clear about who your customer and supplier are. Don't go too big too early. Get a wedge in and widen it. Spotify launched first in Sweden, where labels had lost 60 to 70% of the market and had nothing to lose, and guaranteed them a year of revenue. Evidence from there opened the UK and other markets. The US came three years after launch.
- **Chicken and egg on new platforms:** If you are 1% of a market, asking a supplier to give up 99% of it is a tough sell whatever the economics. Every successful media platform was built on existing content first; originals came later.
- **Don't fight user expectations about price:** Podcasts were expected to be free. Luminary charged for them and struggled. Spotify kept the same value proposition people already expected.
- **Don't borrow mechanics without the system:** Copying a tactic (e.g. "do what Netflix did with originals") almost never works if you don't understand the underlying system. Music originals made no sense for artists (80% of income from touring, they want reach), copyright has compulsory licenses, and it would compete with suppliers.
- **Economic alignment with your suppliers or creators:** Ask what the other side actually earns from and wants. Exclusivity works where creators' business is the content itself, not where they want maximum reach.
- **Look one or two clicks below the data:** At scale, averages hide what's happening. Look at segments and cohorts. "We're all looking at the same data sets... the interesting thing is what happens one or two clicks below."
- **A single health metric tied to the core benefit:** For users, discovery is the core benefit. If a user discovers one item a month they truly love, they will stay a long time.
- **Growth breaks things:** The Dubai chocolate maker chose 10% growth because 30 to 50% would break his systems and force him to replace his people. Fast growth means building processes in anticipation and creating a culture that accepts failure and experimentation.

## Strong quotes
> "The net effect of these three is that it creates this extreme polarization in business where the big gets a lot bigger... But also that there are niches now that all of a sudden can be viable... the thing in the middle, however, ends up being completely vaporized."

> "We don't think the job to be done is different at all, between podcasts and music. It is really the same thing."

> "It was very clear from the beginning to me that the magic experience would be what if you had all the world's music on your hard drive, that's the feeling we wanted to invoke?"

> "Think very, very clearly about who it is you're addressing and not go too big, too early, but actually be incredibly focused on who your customer is and who your supplier is and kind of it's the wedge thing. You get the wedge in and then you widen it over time."

> "If you're 1% of the market and you're asking someone to give up 99% of the market, that's going to be a tough sell, no matter what the economics are."

> "I feel it is because we take the mechanic without understanding the underlying system."

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Job to be done and market definition**
- What job does a customer hire your product to do, in their words, not in industry terms?
- Which jobs that others treat as separate do you think are really the same job? What do you know that makes you sure?
- If you define your market by the job instead of the category, how big is it, and who else is already doing that job today?

**Niche or scale**
- Are you building a profitable niche or going for true global scale? Which one, and why can't you be in the middle?
- If you are going for scale, what share of the real market must you win, and what would a company that size look like?
- Are you sure the market you named is a real market, or a subset of a bigger job someone else will own?

**Competing with free or with the default**
- What do people use today instead of you, even if it is free, bad, or illegal? What exactly is painful about it (time, risk, quality)?
- What is the "all the world's music on your hard drive" feeling for your product? Describe the end experience, then reason backward. Which part is technically hard?
- Are you asking people to pay for something they already expect to get for free? What would it take to change that expectation, and can you afford it?

**Wedge and suppliers**
- What is your wedge: the narrowest customer and supplier you can win first? Why them?
- Which supplier or partner has "nothing to lose" by working with you? What can you guarantee them to make it a win-win?
- What evidence from the wedge will convince the next, bigger partner? How long will that take?
- If you need supply (creators, sellers, content), what share of their market do you represent, and why would they give up the rest for you?
- What do your suppliers or creators actually earn their money from? Does your model help or hurt that?

**Copying and distribution**
- Which part of your plan is borrowed from another company's playbook? Do you understand the system that made it work there, and is your system the same?
- If this is a new consumer app, how will it get distributed when people install about one new app a year? Should it be a feature inside something people already use?

**Signals**
- What is the one thing a user must experience every month to keep coming back? How will you measure it per segment, not on average?

## Red flags / failure modes
- **Stuck in the middle:** Neither a sharp niche nor a credible path to true scale. Ek says the middle gets vaporized.
- **Market defined by an old industry label:** The founder describes the category, not the customer's job, and misses who else does that job.
- **Charging for what people expect free:** Like Luminary with podcasts. Fighting a price expectation is very expensive.
- **Going too big, too early:** Launching everywhere at once instead of winning a narrow wedge and widening it with evidence.
- **Asking suppliers to give up most of their market:** A new platform with 1% of the market asking creators for exclusivity will struggle whatever it pays.
- **Copying a mechanic without the system:** "We'll do Netflix originals for X" without checking if the economics and incentives are the same.
- **Splitting into a separate app too early:** A new standalone consumer app with no distribution plan, when the job could live inside an existing product.
- **Reading only averages:** Decisions based on aggregate numbers that hide very different segments and cohorts.
