## Summary
Gil defines the product manager as the single owner of a product's success. The PM asks and answers the strategy questions: goal, customer, use cases, success metrics, competition, differentiation, channels, business model and price. Weak product orgs only run schedules. Strong ones set vision, make trade-offs and protect the team. A good PRD states who the product is for, what it solves, and what it explicitly does not solve. The "Product to distribution mindset" page adds that a startup wins first by a product so good that people leave the incumbent, then by treating its distribution and customer base as the main asset. For a founder validating an idea, the chapter is a checklist: if you cannot answer the PM's strategy questions, you are not ready to build.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **The PM owns the strategy questions:** What is the goal? Who is the customer? What are the main features and use cases? How do we define success and which metrics track it? What are the competitive dynamics and how do we position? How do we differentiate? What are the key distribution channels? What is the business model and price? Other functions help, but the PM must ask and answer them.
- **Prioritization as trade-offs:** The PM balances design's "pristine, platonic ideal", engineering's "technical pizzazz", sales' "just give me some shit I can sell" and legal's "this may be risky". The job is trade-offs of feature versus impact versus work.
- **The "80% product":** A core PM skill is to decide "the 80% product that should be launched immediately" and the single customer problem it solves.
- **Execution means cutting:** Help hit goals by removing or reprioritizing features, asking "stupid" questions to shorten timelines, and pushing back on extra requests from inside and outside.
- **Execution does not end at launch:** Maintenance, iteration and eventually killing the product are part of the job.
- **Communicate the "why":** The hardest communication is why the roadmap is ordered the way it is. Build a prioritization framework that all functions buy into.
- **Customer contact changes minds:** The PM is a buffer for engineers, but putting an engineer in front of a customer is often the best way to prove a need.
- **PRD questions:** Who are you building this for? What use cases does it meet? What does it solve for and explicitly not solve for? What are the main features? What are the main dependencies?
- **Metrics shape behavior:** "You build what you measure." Even the right metrics can drive the wrong behavior.
- **Strategic sensibility:** How is the industry changing, and how can the product make an end run around competitors? Example: Intel priced chips below cost, volume grew faster than projected, and costs fell to make the low price sustainable.
- **Product first, then distribution:** Step 1, build a product so good customers switch from the incumbent. Step 2, be aggressive about growth early; too many companies get complacent if the product "just works". Step 3, treat customer channels as a core asset and push new products through them. Google paid for toolbar distribution and default search placement despite its "organic" reputation.
- **Retrospectives:** After each launch, review what went well, what went poorly, and why.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Strategy basics**
- In one sentence, what is the goal of this product, and how will you know in 6 months if it worked?
- Who exactly is the customer? Name the person, not the market.
- What is the single customer problem your first version solves? What is the "80% product" you could launch now?
- What does your product explicitly not solve? Write down three things you will refuse to build.
- Which one or two metrics define success? What wrong behavior could those metrics cause?

**Competition and differentiation**
- Who is the incumbent today, and why would someone leave it for you? Is your product clearly better, or just different?
- How will you position against the strongest competitor? What end run around them is possible that they cannot easily copy?

**Business and distribution**
- What is the business model, and what will you charge? Why that price?
- What are your first distribution channels? Do you have a plan for growth, or are you hoping the product "just works"?
- Once you have users, what is the second product you could push through the same channel?

**Trade-offs and execution**
- Which features in your plan are "pristine, platonic" design wishes, which are engineering pizzazz, and which are things a buyer would actually pay for?
- What could you cut today and still solve the core problem?
- Can you explain to a teammate why feature A comes before feature B? What framework did you use?
- When did you or your engineer last sit with a real customer? What changed after that?
- What dependencies (legal, partners, platforms) could block launch?
- How would you sunset this product if it failed? Who would you have to move off it?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The founder talks about schedules, tasks and tools but cannot answer who the customer is, how success is measured, or how the product makes money.
- The "requirements" are a feature list with no statement of who it is for and what it does not do.
- Roadmap order is driven by whoever shouts loudest (one customer, one salesperson, one designer) with no shared prioritization logic.
- Metrics are chosen because they are easy to track, not because they reflect the goal, and nobody asks what behavior they will drive.
- No plan for distribution because "a good product will spread on its own."
- The builders never meet customers; all customer input is filtered secondhand.
- The plan assumes the work ends at launch, with no thought about iteration, support or shutdown.
