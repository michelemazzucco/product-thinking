## Summary
Christensen and co-authors argue that innovation feels like luck because companies do not understand the cause of a purchase. Knowing more about customers (demographics, attributes) and products (features) tells you about correlation, not cause. The cause is a "job": the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. People "hire" products to do jobs and "fire" them when they do the job badly. Jobs have functional, social and emotional dimensions, and the circumstance defines the job. Seeing the job reveals your real competitors (Airbnb competes with not making the trip at all) and the true quality criteria. For idea validation, this asks a founder to define the progress, the circumstance and what gets fired, before talking about the product.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Definition of a job:** The progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. "Progress" means movement toward a goal or aspiration; a job is a process, not a one-off event.
- **Circumstance is part of the job:** A job can only be defined relative to its context. Useful questions: Where are you? When is it? Who are you with? While doing what? What were you doing half an hour ago, and what next? What social, cultural or political pressures apply? Life stage, family and money also matter.
- **Functional, social and emotional dimensions:** The social and emotional dimensions can outweigh the functional one. Childcare example: safety and location matter, but "who will I trust with my children?" weighs more.
- **Hire and fire:** If a product does the job well, people hire it again. If not, they fire it and look for something else.
- **Correlation vs causation:** "The fact that you're 18 to 35 years old with a college degree does not cause you to buy a product." Data is organized by product category or customer category because that is easy to collect, so companies start to believe the market is organized that way.
- **The milkshake:** Surveys of milkshake "type" changed nothing. Watching buyers showed 40% of shakes were bought early morning, to go, by commuters with a long boring drive and one free hand who wanted to stay full until noon. The competitors were bagels, doughnuts and bananas, not other shakes. A separate afternoon job (treat for a child) needed a different, thinner shake.
- **Real competition comes from the job:** Airbnb is inferior to hotels by hotel measures, but it competes with staying with friends or not travelling at all.
- **The illusion of progress:** Companies have stage-gates, six sigma and big R&D budgets, yet innovation stays hit or miss. Activity gives the illusion of progress without causing it.
- **Mini-documentary of the struggle (from Brikman's notes):** What progress is the person trying to make (functional, emotional, social)? What are the circumstances (who, when, where, while doing what)? What obstacles block progress? Are they making do with imperfect solutions? How would they define quality, and what trade-offs would they accept?
- **What a job is not (Brikman):** Not adjectives like "convenience". Not something only one product class can solve ("a chocolate shake in a 12 oz cup"); go up one level of abstraction.
- **Where to find jobs (secondary summaries):** Close to home (your own struggles); nonconsumption (people who buy nothing to solve it); workarounds and "compensating behaviors" (people cobbling together their own fix, a sign they care enough to spend their own time); negative jobs (things people want to avoid); unusual uses of existing products (baking soda in the fridge).
- **What gets fired:** Ask what product a customer must fire to hire yours.
- **Integrate around the job (Brikman, HBSWK):** Build the whole experience, not just the product, around the job. Purpose brands name the job (Milwaukee's Sawzall, Kodak FunSaver). Organizing around jobs is hard for competitors to copy, as with IKEA. Track metrics of the customer's progress (Amazon measures time to delivery, not time to shipment).
- **Org charts hide jobs:** Organizations structured by product or customer category only see opportunities inside that frame. A retailer refused a door-hanging tool because no shelf existed for "the job of hanging a door".

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Defining the job**
- What progress is your customer trying to make? State it as a verb and a noun, not as an adjective like "convenience" or "simple".
- In what exact circumstance does this job arise? Where are they, when, with whom, doing what, and what just happened?
- Could the job be done by products from completely different categories? If only your category can do it, are you describing the product, not the job?
- What are the social and emotional dimensions? What would the customer worry others think? What do they need to feel?
- Does the same person have different jobs at different moments (morning shake vs afternoon treat)? Which one are you building for?

**The real competition**
- What does your customer hire today to make this progress? Include doing nothing, a workaround, or asking a friend.
- What must they fire to hire you? Why would they fire it?
- If you judged your product by the incumbent's quality measures, would it lose? Is that fine because the job is different?
- Who are the nonconsumers: people with this job who use nothing today? Why are they not served?
- Where do you see people building their own workarounds with spreadsheets, scripts or duct tape? How much time do they spend on it?

**Evidence of cause**
- Is your customer definition based on demographics or on circumstance? What evidence shows the circumstance causes the purchase?
- Have you watched people in the moment, like the milkshake researcher, or only asked them what they want?
- What obstacles stop people from making this progress today?
- How would they define "quality" for a better solution, and which trade-offs will they accept to get it?

**Building around the job**
- Beyond the product, what experiences of buying and using it must you design for the job to be done well?
- What metric would show that the customer made progress, not just that you shipped something?
- Could your product's name tell people the job it does?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The target customer is a demographic segment ("millennials", "SMBs 10–50 employees") with no circumstance or job.
- The job is described as a product attribute or an adjective ("faster", "convenient", "all-in-one").
- Competition is defined only as products in the same category; nonconsumption and workarounds are ignored.
- Only functional needs are considered; social and emotional dimensions are missing.
- Research is surveys about desired product features, not observation of what people actually did and why.
- Lots of process and activity, but no clear causal story for why anyone would buy. The illusion of progress.
- The product only fits one shelf or category the market already has, so the job behind it is never named.
