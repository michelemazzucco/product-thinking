## Summary
Ries defines a startup as a human institution building something new under extreme uncertainty. In that setting, building on plan is not progress. The unit of progress is validated learning: data showing the key risks of the business are resolved. Every startup rests on leap-of-faith assumptions, mainly the value hypothesis (does it deliver value once people use it?) and the growth hypothesis (how will it spread?). You test them with a minimum viable product, the version that gives the most validated learning for the least effort. You measure with actionable metrics, not vanity metrics. Then you decide to pivot or persevere. For idea validation, this asks a founder to state their riskiest assumptions, design the cheapest test that could prove them wrong, and say in advance what result would make them change direction.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Startup definition:** "A human institution designed to deliver a new product or service under conditions of extreme uncertainty." A clone of an existing business is not a startup, because its risk can be modeled.
- **The question is "should this be built?":** Not "can it be built?" but "should it be built?" and "can we build a sustainable business around it?"
- **Validated learning is the unit of progress:** Not dollars, code, milestones or products. Validation is data showing the key risks are addressed. A $30,000-revenue startup with clear unit economics can be ahead of a $1M-revenue startup that sells custom one-offs and cannot repeat its sales.
- **Leap-of-faith assumptions: value and growth hypotheses (secondary):** Value hypothesis: does the product deliver value to customers once they use it (does the customer have the problem; does the product solve it)? Growth hypothesis: how will new customers find the product? Zappos tested the value hypothesis by photographing shoes in local stores and buying them only when someone ordered.
- **Build-Measure-Learn loop:** Turn ideas into products, measure how customers respond, learn whether to pivot or persevere. Everything should aim to speed up this loop.
- **Minimum viable product:** "That version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort." MVP is not about minimal products. It needs judgment. IMVU's MVP took six months; another feature took two weeks that "absolutely nobody wanted", and an AdWords smoke test would have shown it sooner.
- **MVP for early adopters:** Early adopters forgive gaps and fill in missing features if the core "tent-pole" features point to the vision.
- **MVP types (secondary):** Smoke test / landing page (offer the product before it exists and see if anyone clicks or pre-orders). Video MVP (Dropbox's demo video drove sign-ups with no product). Concierge MVP (deliver the service by hand, customer knows). Wizard of Oz MVP (customer thinks it is automated, humans do the work).
- **Landing page lesson from IMVU:** They should have built a landing page and run AdWords first. "0% of people would have clicked through", which means the rest of the product did not matter.
- **False negatives and when to stop:** Do not quit after one paper prototype fails. But after many iterations with no uptake and a "yawn" from customers, you are "past the point of minimum viable product. This just isn't a viable product."
- **Vanity vs actionable metrics:** Total hits, total users, billions of messages are vanity: they feel good but do not guide action. Actionable metrics show cause and effect: A/B tests, per-customer and per-segment data, cohort funnels. Three principles: measure what matters (few metrics), metrics are people too (trace numbers to real customers and call them), measure the macro (purchase or retention, not button clicks).
- **Test that could change your mind:** Ask "if this test turns out differently from how I expect, will that cast serious doubts on what I think I know about my customers?" If not, test something bigger.
- **Innovation accounting (secondary):** (1) Use an MVP to establish a real baseline. (2) Tune the engine from baseline toward the ideal, one change at a time. (3) Pivot or persevere. A good pivot makes tuning more productive afterwards.
- **Pivot, don't jump:** A pivot changes direction while keeping one foot in what you learned. Types named in the post: customer segment pivot (same product, new segment), customer problem pivot (same segment, new problem; Starbucks moving from beans to brewed drinks), feature pivot (one feature becomes the product; PayPal and email payments). Jumping to an unrelated vision throws away validated learning.
- **Signs it is time to pivot:** The solution team keeps experimenting, adding features and optimizing the funnel but gets only modest results, cycle after cycle. Then ask: are we solving an important problem? Are early adopters really adopting? Does the product solve the problem we promised?
- **Two dead ends:** "The living dead" (keep spending, hoping the next feature brings traction) and "the compulsive jumper" (never stay long enough to learn).
- **Feedback is for testing the vision:** Find a market for the product as currently specified; do not run a focus group to write the spec. Change the vision only if no market exists for it.
- **Scheduled pivot-or-persevere meetings:** IMVU held a review every two-month cycle with raw data and advisors, so the team could stay heads-down between them.

> "Without the tools to pivot well, startups get stuck between two extremes: the living dead, still expending energy but not really making progress, always hoping the next new feature will cause traction to magically materialize, and the compulsive jumper, never picking a single direction long enough to find out if there's anything there." (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision)

> "A good rule of thumb is to ask yourself, “if this test turns out differently from how I expect, will that cast serious doubts on what I think I know about my customers?” If not, try something bigger." (Vanity Metrics vs. Actionable Metrics)

> "Given a choice between what a successful team has learned and the source code they have produced, I would take the learning every time." (Validated learning about customers)

> "Startups exist not to make stuff, make money, or serve customers. They exist to learn how to build a sustainable business." (theleanstartup.com principles)

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Leap-of-faith assumptions**
- What are the two or three assumptions that, if wrong, kill this idea? Which one is riskiest?
- Value hypothesis: once someone uses this, what proves it delivers value to them? What would you see them do?
- Growth hypothesis: how will the next 100 customers find you? Word of mouth, paid ads, sales, a viral loop? Why do you believe that?
- Is this really uncertain, or could you model it like a known business? If it is a clone, why treat it as a startup?

**The MVP**
- What is the least-effort thing you could put in front of real customers this week that tests the riskiest assumption?
- Could a landing page, smoke test or pre-order show whether anyone wants it before you build it? What click or pre-order rate would make you stop?
- Could you deliver the outcome by hand (concierge) or fake the automation (Wizard of Oz) before writing code?
- What will you learn from your MVP that you do not know now? If the answer is "whether it works", be more specific.
- Who are the early adopters who will forgive missing features? What are the tent-pole features they must have?

**Measuring**
- What metric will tell you the business is working? Is it per-customer and tied to a decision, or a total that only goes up?
- If your numbers rise next week, how will you know it was your change and not a random spike?
- What will you do differently if the result is the opposite of what you expect? If nothing, why run the test?
- What do you know about unit economics: what does a customer cost to acquire, and what do they bring in?

**Pivot or persevere**
- Before you start, what result would make you pivot? Write it down now.
- How many cycles of "modest results" will you accept before you question the problem, not the execution?
- Are your early adopters actually adopting, or just signing up?
- If you had to pivot, what would you keep: the segment, the problem, or one feature that people already use?
- When is your next scheduled pivot-or-persevere review, and who will challenge you with the raw data?

## Red flags / failure modes
- Months spent building before any real customer sees even a rough version.
- Progress is reported as features shipped, code written or total revenue, not as validated learning about customers.
- Success is reported with vanity metrics (total sign-ups, page views, cumulative users) with no cohort or per-customer view.
- Revenue comes from custom founder-led deals that cannot be repeated or delegated.
- The founder has no stated assumptions, or no result that would make them change course.
- Tests are designed so they cannot fail, or only confirm what the founder already believes.
- "Living dead": each cycle adds features hoping traction will appear, with flat results.
- "Compulsive jumper": switches to an unrelated idea after every disappointment, keeping none of what was learned.
- Customer feedback is used to write the spec, instead of to test whether the vision has a market.
