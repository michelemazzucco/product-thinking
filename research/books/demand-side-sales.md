## Summary
Moesta argues that selling should start from the buyer's side: what struggle in their life causes them to buy. Demand is caused by a "struggling moment", not by demographics or product features. "If there's no struggle, there's no demand." Every switch is governed by four forces: push of the situation and pull of the new solution drive change; anxiety about the new and habit of the present hold it back. Buyers move through a timeline from first thought to ongoing use, and each phase needs a different kind of help. You learn all of this by interviewing people who already bought, digging for causes, not opinions. For idea validation, the test is blunt: can you point to real people in a real struggling moment who already tried to make progress, and what stopped or moved them?

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Supply side vs demand side:** Supply side starts with the product, its features, and "who needs my product?", using correlated demographic data. Demand side starts with the buyer's struggle, the progress they want, and what they will trade off. Demand is independent of supply.
- **Causation, not correlation:** From his mentor Genichi Taguchi. Demographics correlate with purchase but do not cause it. There are only a few sets of causes (four or five jobs) behind why people buy a given product.
- **Struggling moment:** The point when someone realizes something could be better. No struggle, no demand. People with a vague struggle and no picture of progress will "bitch but not switch". Show them what progress looks like.
- **Progress = start + end:** Value is relative to where the person starts. You need both the circumstance and the desired outcome. Eliminating the struggle is not progress; overcoming it is.
- **Ask "why" several times:** Drill → hole → plug → lamp → "it's hard to see, and I want to read better." The real job may need a different product entirely (a Kindle).
- **Three sources of energy:** Functional, emotional and social motivations.
- **Four forces of progress:**
  - Push: the situation that makes the person say "I need to do something different".
  - Pull: the attraction of the new solution and the vision of life with it. There is no pull until they see a possible solution. Pull without push goes nowhere.
  - Anxiety: self-doubt and fear about the new solution ("will it work?", "how do I return it?"). More features can raise anxiety as much as pull, so they cancel out.
  - Habit: attachment to the old way ("but I really like my Egg McMuffin").
  - Reducing anxiety often beats adding pull: money-back guarantees, easy returns (Kohl's, Zappos), and in the condo case, including moving and two years of storage instead of free granite.
- **Eight elements of a job (Re-Wired guide):** Context; struggling moments; pushes and pulls; anxieties and habits; desired outcomes; hiring and firing criteria; key trade-offs; basic quality of the job. Milkshake example: long boring commute, one free hand, must last 20 minutes; firing criterion is "too sweet, feels like dessert for breakfast".
- **Buying timeline (six phases):** First thought (space opens in the mind for a solution); passive looking (learning, framing, not yet acting); active looking (seeing options, ruling things in and out); deciding (trade-offs, group buy-in, setting expectations); onboarding (first use, seeing progress); ongoing use (new habits, new struggles). Sales, marketing and support must meet the buyer at the right phase. Pushing a decision on someone in active looking makes them anxious.
- **Real competitive set:** Snickers competes with a sandwich, Red Bull and coffee (hunger, keep going). Milky Way competes with ice cream, brownies and wine (emotional reward). They do not compete with each other.
- **Interview principles:** Humility (you are not smart enough to know why people buy). Causality (nothing is random; there are no impulse buys). Tradeoffs (everyone gives up something). Disconnected (people do not know why they do things; dig past the "cake layer" of the story they tell themselves). Unpack vague words like "healthy". Do not interview alone. Ten to twenty interviews of recent buyers with varied backgrounds is enough.
- **A job is not a persona or a demographic:** Jobs are moment-in-time specific. Only people have jobs, even in B2B: find the person who spends personal capital to make the purchase happen.
- **Right level of abstraction:** Too high and you think everyone wants it; too low and it applies to two or three people. Aim for three to five clear jobs.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**The struggling moment**
- Describe the last time a real person hit the struggle your product solves. What day was it, what happened, what did they do next?
- What made that moment the day they said "today's the day"? What changed in their life?
- Is this a real struggle, or something people complain about but never act on? Who has actually tried to fix it?
- What does progress look like for them? Can they picture it, or do you have to show them?

**The four forces**
- What pushes people out of their current solution? Is that push strong and frequent, or mild and rare?
- What pulls them toward yours? Is that pull tied to a push they already feel, or only to how cool the product looks?
- What will make them anxious about switching to you: cost, learning, data migration, looking foolish, losing what works?
- What habit must they give up? What do they love about the old way?
- If you added three more features, would anxiety rise as much as attraction? What could you remove or guarantee instead?

**Who and why**
- Have you interviewed people who recently switched to something to solve this, including to a competitor? What caused it?
- When a buyer says "I want to be more productive" or "healthier", what do they actually mean? Have you unpacked their words?
- What do your buyers fire to hire you? Name the product, habit or workaround.
- What does your real competitive set look like from the buyer's side, not your category's?
- In a B2B sale, who is the person spending their personal capital to bring you in, and what job do they have?
- What trade-offs are buyers willing to make? What are they not willing to give up?

**The timeline**
- Where are most of your prospects on the timeline: first thought, passive looking, active looking, or deciding? How do you know?
- What would help someone in passive looking move to active looking, without pushing a decision too early?
- After the first use, how will the buyer see that they made progress?

## Red flags / failure modes
- Customers are described by demographics or personas, not by circumstances and struggling moments.
- No interviews with people who actually bought or switched; evidence is opinions about a future product.
- The founder believes in "impulse buys" or random demand and cannot explain what causes a purchase.
- The pitch is a list of features and benefits (supply side), not the progress the buyer wants.
- The plan to grow is "add more features", with no thought for anxiety or habit holding people back.
- The struggle is real but people "bitch but don't switch", and the product does not show them what progress looks like.
- The job is defined too broadly ("everyone wants to save time") or so narrowly it fits only a few people.
- Sales pushes a decision on people who are still passively looking.
