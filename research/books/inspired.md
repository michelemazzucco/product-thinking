## Summary
Cagan's core claim is that most product ideas fail, and that strong teams find out which ones before engineering builds them. Every idea carries four risks: value, usability, feasibility and business viability. Teams should tackle the big ones, especially value and viability, early and cheaply through product discovery, using prototypes and real users, not full releases. Before discovery, a quick opportunity assessment answers ten questions, starting with "exactly what problem will this solve?" The point is to kill weak ideas before money is spent and to know what success will require for strong ones. For a founder, this is a direct structure for a validation interview: name the risks, rank them, and show evidence for each.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **The four big risks:**
  - Value risk: will customers buy it, or users choose to use it?
  - Usability risk: can users figure out how to use it?
  - Feasibility risk: can engineers build it with the time, skills and technology available?
  - Business viability risk: does it work for the business? Includes fit with go-to-market or sales channel, partner contracts and legal compliance, affordable customer acquisition, ability to monetize, and fit with the brand promise.
- **Value is usually the hardest, viability is the most ignored:** Cagan split "valuable" into value and viability in the 2nd edition because PMs focused on customer value and overlooked business value. A product customers love but the business cannot support still fails.
- **Who owns which risk:** PM owns value and viability (and overall outcomes). Designer owns usability. Lead engineer owns feasibility.
- **Three ways strong teams moved beyond Lean and Agile:** Tackle big risks early; figure out solutions collaboratively (product, design, engineering side by side); focus on solving problems and results, not features and roadmaps.
- **Product opportunity assessment (10 questions):**
  1. Exactly what problem will this solve? (value proposition)
  2. For whom do we solve that problem? (target market)
  3. How big is the opportunity? (market size)
  4. What alternatives are out there? (competitive landscape)
  5. Why are we best suited to pursue this? (our differentiator)
  6. Why now? (market window)
  7. How will we get this product to market? (go-to-market strategy)
  8. How will we measure success/make money from this product? (metrics/revenue strategy)
  9. What factors are critical to success? (solution requirements)
  10. Given the above, what's the recommendation? (go or no-go)
- **Purpose of the assessment:** Either stop the company wasting time and money on a poor opportunity, or, for good ones, understand what success will require. The first question is the hardest: most PMs answer with a list of features, not a crisp problem statement. Be conservative on market size; not every opportunity needs to be a billion-dollar market. Go-to-market can change the product requirements.
- **Bad sources of ideas:** Ideas that come "down from above", or a large customer offering to fund a "special", are poor bases for a product.
- **Product discovery has two parts:** (1) discover whether real users want this, i.e. validate the opportunity; (2) discover a solution that is usable, useful and feasible. Market discovery is often easier than solution discovery.
- **Discovery is not a fixed phase:** Scheduling discovery like building a house fails. If users are not excited or cannot use the prototype, you must change course, not ship anyway.
- **Expensive prototypes:** Most companies use full engineering teams and full releases as discovery. They take three or more releases over one to two years to get something usable, using live customers as test subjects. Startups often cannot survive that long.
- **Root causes of failure (talk recap, secondary):** Wrong sources of ideas, roadmaps of features, and focusing on outputs over outcomes.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Value risk**
- Exactly what problem does this solve? Say it in one sentence without naming a single feature.
- For whom, specifically? Who has this problem badly enough to change what they do today?
- What evidence do you have that they will buy it or choose to use it, beyond them saying it sounds nice?
- What alternatives do they use now, and why would they switch?

**Usability and feasibility risk**
- Have you watched a real user try a prototype without your help? Where did they get stuck?
- What part of this might be much harder to build than you think? Have you asked an engineer to try the riskiest piece?

**Business viability risk**
- How will you reach customers, and does the product fit that channel? Could the channel change what you must build?
- Can you afford to acquire a customer? What do you think a customer costs to get, and what will they pay you over time?
- How exactly will this make money? What would make a customer stop paying?
- Are there legal, compliance, partner or platform rules that could block or reshape this?
- Does this fit the promise your brand or company already makes?

**Opportunity assessment**
- How big is the opportunity, estimated conservatively and bottom-up? Does it need to be big?
- Why are you the right people to build this? What do you have that others don't?
- Why now? What changed that makes this possible or needed today and not five years ago?
- What factors are critical to success, and which of them do you not control?
- If you had to write "go" or "no-go" today, which would you write, and what would change your answer?

**Discovery discipline**
- Which of the four risks is biggest for this idea? What is the cheapest test that would reduce it this week?
- Is your plan to learn with prototypes, or to learn by shipping full releases to real customers?
- When your last test disappointed you, what did you change? Or did you build it anyway because the timeline was set?
- Where did this idea come from: a real customer problem, your own opinion, a boss, or one large customer asking for a special?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The answer to "what problem does it solve?" is a list of features.
- Only value risk is considered; no one has looked at acquisition cost, channel fit, monetization, legal or brand constraints.
- The plan uses engineering releases as the learning method: build first, then see what happens with live customers.
- Discovery is time-boxed to fit an engineering schedule, and the product ships even when prototype tests were negative.
- The idea came from above or from one big customer willing to pay for a custom build.
- Market size is optimistic top-down guesswork, and the idea only works if it is huge.
- No clear "go / no-go" moment; the team is committed before the assessment.
- A roadmap of features and outputs replaces outcomes the product must achieve.
