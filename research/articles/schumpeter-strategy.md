# Schumpeter on Strategy — Jerry Neumann

## Source
- **Title:** Schumpeter on Strategy (Reaction Wheel, January 18, 2019)
- **Speaker / author:** Jerry Neumann (venture investor), summarizing Joseph Schumpeter's *Theory of Economic Development* (chapter "Entrepreneurial Profit")
- **URL:** https://reactionwheel.net/2019/01/schumpeter-on-strategy.html
- **Type:** article
- **Notes on source quality:** Full text read. It is Neumann's own recap of Schumpeter, not Schumpeter's words. The three diagrams (income by decile, excess profit decay curve, moat extension) were images and not visible; their meaning is clear from the prose. Data on founder income is from Scott Shane, *The Illusions of Entrepreneurship* (2008).

## Summary
Neumann restates Schumpeter's model of where profit comes from. In a competitive market, surplus profit is competed away, so most founders earn the same as they would as employees. Excess profit (entrepreneurial profit) only comes from innovation: getting inputs cheaper or selling outputs for more. That profit then decays as imitators copy you. Strategy is about how big the innovation is and how long you can delay the decay. This maps to Porter's cost leadership, differentiation and focus. Neumann also splits startups into two kinds: job-replacement businesses and innovation businesses. For idea validation, this forces a founder to say where the excess value comes from, how long it lasts, and which kind of company they are really building.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Profit gets competed away:** If a company uses the same inputs and makes the same outputs as competitors, costs and prices match. Any surplus above the risk-adjusted cost of capital attracts price cuts or new entrants until it is gone. The founder is just another input, paid like a manager.
- **Most entrepreneurs earn employee wages:** Shane's data: for the middle eight income deciles, entrepreneurs and employees earn the same. Median owner-managed firm revenue is $90,000. 81% of founders have no desire to grow. Many start in crowded industries and report no competitive advantage.
- **Two sources of excess profit:** Innovation that lowers input cost for the same output (efficiency innovation), or innovation that creates a better or different output you can sell for more (value innovation), or both.
- **Excess profit decays:** Imitators enter and wear the surplus away. Total value created is the area under the decaying excess-profit curve. That value is split between founders, employees and financiers.
- **Maps to Porter:** Cost leadership and differentiation are Schumpeter's two sources. Focus means picking a sector to reach one of them. A "competitive advantage" is an innovation that enables one of these.
- **Ways to slow the decay (moats):** Regulation and intellectual property (Neumann counts brand here). Continuous improvement and learning by doing (the experience curve). Positioning: segmenting the market so competitors do not directly compete, including "blue oceans" (find an unexploited combination of attributes you can excel at; zero competition "for a little while").
- **Portfolio of innovations:** At company level, have the next source of excess profit ready before the current one runs out (BCG growth-share matrix).
- **Two kinds of startups:** Job-replacement startups mainly benefit the founder (self-direction, doing what you love). Innovation startups create most new jobs and products. Both are valid, but they need different expectations, funding and policy.

## Strong quotes
> "80% of entrepreneurs make the same amount of money they would if they were employed."

> "Those that make money, an entrepreneurial profit, do so by breaking the status quo. They innovate. They either get their inputs for less or they sell their outputs for more."

> "Competitors figure out that there is this extra money and they imitate the innovator."

> "Just enumerate the various attributes your product can meaningfully differ on and find an unexploited combination that your company can excel at. Voila, zero competition…for a little while."

> "Most people start businesses simply because they don’t like working for someone else." (Scott Shane, quoted by Neumann)

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Where the value comes from**
- What is your innovation, in one line: do you produce the same thing cheaper, or something better or different that people will pay more for?
- If it is cheaper: what input do you get for less than incumbents, and why can't they get it too?
- If it is better: what will customers pay more for, and how much more? How do you know?
- If you used the same inputs and made the same outputs as competitors, what would be left? Is that your actual plan?

**How long it lasts**
- When a competitor sees your margins, how long until they copy you? What exactly would they copy first?
- What slows that decay for you: IP, regulation, brand, learning by doing, or a position competitors do not want?
- Are you on an experience curve, where each customer makes you better or cheaper in a way a newcomer cannot match quickly?
- What unexploited combination of attributes are you choosing? Why will that space stay empty for long?
- What is your next innovation when this one is competed away?

**What kind of company this is**
- Are you building a job for yourself, or a company that captures excess profit from innovation? Be honest. Both are fine, but they need different plans.
- If you do not have a competitive advantage, how will you earn more than you would as an employee?
- Are you entering an industry that already has many similar firms? What makes you different from them?
- Who gets the excess value you create: you, your customers, your suppliers, or your investors?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The idea uses the same inputs and produces the same output as existing businesses, with no cost or value innovation.
- The founder cannot say whether the advantage is lower cost or higher value.
- No answer to "what happens when someone copies this?" or the answer is "we'll execute better."
- A crowded market and no stated competitive advantage, yet the plan assumes venture-style returns.
- Treating a lifestyle or job-replacement business as a high-growth startup (or raising venture money for one).
- The moat is assumed to be permanent; no thought about decay or the next innovation.
