# The Eleven Laws of Showrunning — Javier Grillo-Marxuach

## Source
- **Title:** The Eleven Laws of Showrunning (dated 1.20.16)
- **Speaker / author:** Javier Grillo-Marxuach (TV writer/producer; showrunner of The Middleman)
- **URL:** https://okbjgm.weebly.com/uploads/3/1/5/0/31506003/11_laws_of_showrunning_nice_version.pdf
- **Type:** article
- **Notes on source quality:** Full 25-page PDF read. It is about running a TV writers room, not about products. The mapping to product leadership below is our interpretation; the laws and quotes are his. Most useful for testing whether a founder can define the product clearly, decide fast, and treat early versions as proof of concept.

## Summary
Grillo-Marxuach argues that TV shows fail as workplaces because showrunners hide behind "my creative process" instead of managing. Every law asks the leader to give up some ego to serve the show and the team. The core ideas: know what your show is and say it clearly; never leave people guessing what to do next; decide early; judge ideas at the sketch stage; ship drafts fast because a script is a proof of concept; stay in the room where the core work happens; tell the truth early. For a founder, this maps directly to product leadership. A founder who cannot state what the product is, who waits to "know it when I see it", or who hides in polish work is not ready to build with a team.

## Key ideas and frameworks
The eleven laws, with how each maps to product work:
- **1. It's all about you; stop making it all about you:** You own the outcome, but the team works for pay, not for your genius. Their loyalty must be earned by giving them room inside your framework. *Product:* the founder owns the vision but must not hoard information or make people compete for favor.
- **2. Know your show and tell everyone what it is:** The pilot was a prototype. You must figure out what worked and turn it into a "reproducible result", then define tone, story and characters for everyone. Never say "I'll know it when I see it." *Product:* write down what the product is and is not, precisely, and repeat it until everyone can say it.
- **3. Always describe a path to success:** Do not end a meeting without each person knowing what to deliver next. Even "help me figure out the next hill to take" is a directive. *Product:* every review ends with clear next steps and a defined problem, not a vague "keep exploring".
- **4. Make decisions early and often:** Decision aversion comes from fear of being judged. It makes staff build contingencies, and decision-averse managers usually end up picking one of the first options pitched anyway. *Product:* commit, then let the team find out what is achievable.
- **5. Do not demand a final product at the idea stage:** Architects see buildings from blueprints. You must judge stories from the board, outline and draft, not demand polish before you can evaluate. *Product:* learn to judge sketches, prototypes and rough MVPs. Demanding polish early kills the team's process and wastes time.
- **6. Write and rewrite quickly:** A script is a work order and a "proof of concept". If it will fail, know sooner. Most of the time it will only be "ready enough". Best ideas survive criticism; do not over-defend weak ones. *Product:* ship the spec or the build fast; the artifact is how you test and communicate the vision.
- **7. Track multiple targets by delegating:** Six stories are always in flight, from idea to post-production. Train your writers to carry the vision into all the meetings, and guard your time in the writers room, "the forge of your show's creation". *Product:* delegate the many side meetings; protect your time on the core problem.
- **8. Resist the siren call of the "sexy glamorous jobs":** Costumes, casting, VFX and the editing suite are fun and feel like work, but they distract from story. *Product:* logo, branding, pixel polish and tooling feel productive; if you are fixing the product in polish, you lost it in the problem definition.
- **9. Expect staff to perform at varying levels of competence:** Teach junior people with specific feedback. Do not let trusted outsiders (spouses, assistants, a line cook) override senior staff. Handle "Doctor No" (objects without solutions; answer "You break it, you bought it"), "Hostage Taker" and "Politician" types directly. *Product:* weigh feedback by who it comes from; demand that critics offer a fix.
- **10. Deliver good and bad news early and often:** Secrets are poison. Transparency streamlines your life and keeps you in control of the narrative. Backers are partners, not enemies. *Product:* tell the team and investors bad results early; do not hide failed tests.
- **11. Share credit for success to a fault:** Credit is not finite. Never throw a team member under the bus; say "There's still work to be done" and own the blame.
- **Epilogue, the fortress of "my creativity":** Leaders retreat into "my process" to avoid hard responsibility. "Simple" does not mean "easy": each law asks for a painful concession to an unpleasant truth.

## Strong quotes
> "To say "I'll know it when I see it" is to abdicate the hard work of creation while hoarding the authority to declare what is or isn't good."

> "The pilot episode was a prototype. Now you have to discern what it was that worked so well in the pilot and turn that into a reproducible result."

> "A script is your proof of concept, and if its fate is to fail that proof, then you are better off knowing sooner rather than later, so that you - and all of your employees - can use the time to fix what's broken and right the ship while there is still time."

> "If you repeatedly find yourself "looking for the show" in post, it is because you most likely lost it in the writers room."

> "The worst position for a leader is as the bearer of bad news everyone already knows."

> "If you can pitch an objection, but not a solution, you have not earned the right to speak."

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Knowing what the product is**
- Can you describe what this product is, and what it is not, in a way a new hire could repeat without you in the room?
- If you showed a teammate three mockups, could you say in advance which one fits the vision, and why? Or would you "know it when you see it"?
- What was your "pilot"? What exactly worked in it, and how will you make that repeatable rather than a lucky one-off?
- What did not work in your first version? Have you said it out loud, with honesty?

**Deciding and shipping**
- What is the one decision you have been putting off about this product? What would it cost to make it today?
- Are you waiting for more data, or are you afraid of being judged once you commit?
- What is the roughest artifact (sketch, landing page, script, prototype) that would let you test the core idea this week?
- Are you asking for a polished product before you are willing to judge the idea? Can you evaluate a blueprint?
- Which of your ideas are you defending out of attachment rather than evidence? Would it survive criticism from someone you respect?

**Where your time goes**
- How much of your last two weeks went to the "sexy glamorous jobs" (brand, logo, tooling, polish) versus the core problem?
- If you keep tweaking the finished UI to make it feel right, did you lose the product in the problem definition?
- Which side tasks could someone else carry, so you can stay in the "writers room" of the product?

**Feedback and truth**
- Whose feedback are you weighting most? Are they people who will question you productively, or people too close or too junior to push back?
- When someone says your idea won't work, do you ask them for an alternative?
- What bad news about this idea do your teammates or advisors already know that you have not said yet?
- What would you tell an investor today about the weakest part of the plan?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The founder cannot define the product and says some form of "I'll know it when I see it."
- Meetings end without clear owners and next deliverables; the team has to guess what the founder wants.
- Repeated delay on key decisions, then a return to one of the first options proposed.
- Demanding a finished, polished product before judging whether the idea works.
- Time pours into branding, polish, tooling or re-editing instead of the core problem.
- Feedback from friends, family or random "trusted outsiders" overrides people with real expertise or real customers.
- Bad news (failed tests, missed targets) is hidden until everyone already knows.
- Hiding behind "my process" or "my vision" to avoid hard trade-offs.
