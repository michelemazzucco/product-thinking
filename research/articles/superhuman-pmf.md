# How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product/Market Fit — Rahul Vohra

## Source
- **Title:** How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product Market Fit (First Round Review, November 12, 2018)
- **Speaker / author:** Rahul Vohra, founder and CEO of Superhuman
- **URL:** https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/
- **Type:** article
- **Notes on source quality:** Full text read. Charts and tables (segment breakdowns, word clouds) are images and were not visible; numbers below come from the prose. Written by the founder about his own company, so it is a success story with survivorship bias. The survey method is borrowed from Sean Ellis.

## Summary
Vohra spent two years building Superhuman pre-launch and had no way to tell the team they were not ready. Existing PMF definitions (Andreessen, Graham, Altman) are lagging indicators. He adopted Sean Ellis's leading indicator: ask users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product, and track the share who say "very disappointed". The benchmark is 40%. Superhuman started at 22%. Through a four-step engine (segment, analyze, build the roadmap, repeat) it reached 33% by segmenting and 58% within three quarters. For idea validation, this gives a founder a concrete test of whether anyone truly needs the product, and a disciplined way to decide whose feedback to ignore.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Lagging vs leading indicator:** Revenue growth, press and investor interest only show PMF after you have it. The "very disappointed" percentage is measurable before launch.
- **The Sean Ellis test and 40% benchmark:** Ellis benchmarked nearly a hundred startups. Those that struggled almost always had under 40% "very disappointed"; those with strong traction almost always exceeded it. Slack scored 51% in Hiten Shah's survey of 731 users.
- **Who to survey:** Users who recently experienced the core product, using Ellis's rule of at least two uses in the last two weeks. Results become directionally correct around 40 respondents. Do not survey anyone twice.
- **The four survey questions:**
  1. How would you feel if you could no longer use Superhuman? (A) Very disappointed (B) Somewhat disappointed (C) Not disappointed
  2. What type of people do you think would most benefit from Superhuman?
  3. What is the main benefit you receive from Superhuman?
  4. How can we improve Superhuman for you?
- **Step 1, segment to find supporters:** Give each respondent a persona. Look at which personas appear in the "very disappointed" group (founders, managers, executives, business development) and narrow the market to them. This alone raised the score from 22% to 33%.
- **High-expectation customer (HXC):** From Julie Supan. The most discerning person in the target group, who enjoys the main benefit and spreads the word. Use answers to Q2 from very-disappointed users: happy users describe themselves, in the words that matter to them. Superhuman's HXC was "Nicole", a busy professional who reads 100–200 emails a day, prides herself on responsiveness, and is skeptical that any tool can make her faster.
- **Narrow is fine:** Quoting Paul Graham, build something a small number of people want a large amount, not something many people want a little.
- **Step 2, analyze feedback:** Ask why people love it (Q3 from very disappointed: speed, focus, keyboard shortcuts) and what holds others back (Q4). Politely ignore "not disappointed" users entirely. Split "somewhat disappointed" users by whether the main benefit (speed) resonates. Ignore those for whom it does not. Listen closely to those for whom it does: something small holds them back. For Superhuman that was mainly a mobile app, then integrations, attachments, calendaring, unified inbox, search, read receipts.
- **Step 3, split the roadmap:** Half on doubling down on what lovers love (more speed, more shortcuts, more automation, design touches). Half on fixing what holds back the on-the-fence users. Doing only the first will not raise the score; doing only the second lets competitors overtake you. Rank with simple low/medium/high cost vs impact. Impact for the "hold back" half is clear from request counts; for the "love" half it takes product instinct.
- **Step 4, repeat and make it the top metric:** Survey new users continuously, track weekly, monthly, quarterly. The only key result of the product team's OKR was the very-disappointed percentage. Rebuild the roadmap every quarter. Expect the score to drop as you reach less forgiving mainstream users.
- **Do not grow before fit:** Investors should not push growth before PMF. Once you hit the target, push hard on growth.

## Strong quotes
> "The product-market fit definitions I had found were vivid and compelling, but they were lagging indicators — by the time investment bankers are staking out your house, you already have product-market fit."

> "This is a very powerful question, as happy users will almost always describe themselves, not other people, using the words that matter to them."

> "This batch of not disappointed users should not impact your product strategy in any way. They’ll request distracting features, present ill-fitting use cases and probably be very vocal, all before they churn out and leave you with a mangled, muddled roadmap."

> "If you only double down on what users love, your product-market fit score won’t increase. If you only address what holds users back, your competition will likely overtake you."

> "Common practice would be to "throw it out there and see what sticks,” which may be fine after a few months of effort when the sunk cost is low. But the “launch and see what happens” method seemed irresponsible and reckless to me — especially given the years that that we had invested."

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Measuring real need**
- If your product disappeared tomorrow, what percentage of your active users would be "very disappointed"? Have you asked them?
- Do you have at least 40 people who have used the core product twice in the last two weeks? If not, what does that tell you?
- Which signal are you relying on today, a leading one you can measure now, or a lagging one like revenue or press?

**Who it is really for**
- Among people who would be very disappointed, what do they have in common? Job, situation, volume of the problem?
- Can you describe your high-expectation customer as specifically as "Nicole": what she does all day, how often she hits the problem, what she is skeptical of?
- When your best users describe who would benefit, what exact words do they use? Do those words appear in your pitch?
- Are you willing to narrow to that small group and ignore everyone else for now?

**Whose feedback counts**
- Which feedback are you acting on from people who would not miss the product at all? What happens if you drop it entirely?
- For people who are "somewhat disappointed", does your main benefit resonate with them? If not, why are you building for them?
- What is the one main benefit your biggest fans name, unprompted? If you cannot name it, what are you doubling down on?
- What single thing holds back the users who like your main benefit but are not yet fans?

**Roadmap discipline**
- How much of your roadmap deepens what fans love, and how much removes blockers for near-fans? Is it close to half and half?
- Is your top metric one that tells you if people need the product, or one that tells you if people are signing up?
- Are you being pushed (by investors or yourself) to grow before you have evidence of fit?

## Red flags / failure modes
- No users who have used the core product repeatedly; "validation" is sign-ups, waitlists or compliments.
- Fewer than 40% of engaged users would be very disappointed without the product, and the founder still plans to scale.
- The target market is defined by the founder's assumptions, not by who actually loves the product.
- The roadmap is driven by the loudest users, including people who would not miss the product.
- The founder cannot name the main benefit that fans value, or fans name different benefits.
- The team only polishes what fans love and ignores blockers, or only chases requested features and loses the core.
- Growth spending starts before there is a measurable sign of product-market fit.
- Surveying the same users repeatedly, or surveying people who never really used the product.
