# Business strategy with Hamilton Helmer (7 Powers)

## Source
- **Title:** Business strategy with Hamilton Helmer (7 Powers)
- **Speaker / author:** Hamilton Helmer (author of 7 Powers, Strategy Capital), interviewed by Lenny Rachitsky
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hKq1_KPSqy0
- **Type:** transcript
- **Notes on source quality:** Clean transcript with a few "[inaudible]" marks and sponsor reads. The episode is shorter than most. Helmer and Lenny deliberately skip defining all seven powers ("go read the book"). Only some powers get a real explanation here. About the last fifth is a tangent on US debt and the lightning round.

## Summary
Helmer defines power as a structure that gives a business durable, above-normal returns: a benefit (lower cost or higher price) plus a barrier (competitors cannot copy it). Strategy, in his narrow useful sense, is about the long-term determinants of business value. He now thinks founders should think about strategy always, even before product-market fit, to "tilt probabilities" toward power. For startups, only four powers are realistically available, and they come in sequence: counter positioning first, then scale economies, switching costs and network economies. Most claimed moats in decks (brand, data scale, flywheels, fast execution) are not material power. For idea validation, this source forces the founder to say why an incumbent cannot simply copy the idea, and whether any advantage is a castle or a shack.

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Power = benefit + barrier:** The benefit is a cost or price advantage that lifts margins. The barrier is what stops competitors from taking it away over time. He calls this the "to be or not to be test." A moat is only the barrier. You can have a moat around worthless land.
- **Castle or shack:** Ask whether what you built is truly protected. Netflix spent huge effort on UI and recommendations, but Blockbuster copied the UI until you could not tell them apart. Important work is not the same as power.
- **Strategy is about value, and value is long-term:** Business value is the NPV of expected cash flow, so strategy has a long time constant. Tactics are short. Pearl Harbor was a tactical win and a strategic disaster.
- **Think about power always:** He used to believe "product-market fit first, then strategy." He changed his mind. Early on, ask which characteristics of a business proposition tilt it toward the availability of power. After PMF, power defines competitive position. In stability, you must know your power to defend it.
- **Three phases of a business:** Origination, takeoff, stability. Different powers are available at different phases ("power progression").
- **Powers not available to startups:** Branding and process power only arrive in the stability phase. Paying for a Super Bowl ad buys brand recognition, not brand power. Cornered resource (an asset valuable to anyone, protected by law or others' lack of knowledge, like a drug patent) is a separate, obvious class.
- **The four startup powers, in sequence:** Counter positioning comes first, because product-market fit is usually a substitution of an existing need in a novel way. Without counter positioning, an incumbent can extend its product and crush you. Later come scale economies, switching costs and network economies, which depend on your scale relative to competitors.
- **Scale economies:** Spread a big fixed cost over more units. Netflix spreads content cost (about 50% of costs) over more subscribers. The curve flattens, so data scale advantages are rare: competitors are often close enough in scale.
- **Network effects vs network economies:** A network economy must be material, big enough to create a real price or margin difference. Most "flywheels" exist but are not material. Uber and Lyft have network effects but, he suspects, no network economies. Uber's edge is a modest, geographically specific scale economy plus a war of attrition. Uber first misdefined its business as international.
- **Operational excellence is not power (usually):** It can be copied by hiring consultants or competitors' staff. It is essential in the takeoff phase to win share. It is a treadmill: stop running and you get creamed. It is only power when it is material and opaque (TSMC fabs, Toyota).
- **Value comes from three things:** Power, market size, and operational excellence. Everything else falls into these.
- **AI:** No eighth power yet. AI raises questions about scale economies (huge model costs), network economies (does one user's use improve another's?), and switching costs (a model that learns you). He expects most AI impact in businesses that existed before and will after, like chips in cars.
- **Action is the first principle:** Do things. Theory gives guideposts, not answers.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Counter positioning and incumbents**
- What existing need are you substituting for, and who serves it today?
- Why can't the biggest incumbent just add your idea as a feature next quarter? Be specific about what stops them.
- What would the incumbent have to give up or damage in their own business to copy you? If nothing, what is your refuge?
- What new technology makes your approach possible now, and does it help the incumbent just as much?

**Benefit and barrier**
- In one sentence, what is your benefit: will you have lower cost or be able to charge a higher price than rivals?
- Now the barrier: why will that advantage still exist in five years when competitors see it working?
- List what you think is your moat. For each item, is it a castle or a shack? Could a well-funded copycat mimic it in a year?
- If your answer is "great team" or "we move fastest," what happens when you stop running on that treadmill?

**Scale, networks, switching**
- If you claim a network effect, does one more user make the product materially better for other users? How big is that effect in money, not in theory?
- If you claim a data advantage, at what point does more data stop making the product better? Will rivals reach that point too?
- Where is your big fixed cost that more customers would spread thinner? Is your scale advantage local (like a city) or global?
- What would it cost a customer, in money, time or risk, to switch away from you after a year?

**Defining the business**
- How have you defined your market? Could you be misdefining it, like Uber calling a city-by-city business "international transportation"?
- Is your brand advantage something you earned over time, or something you are planning to buy with ads?

**Strategy before PMF**
- Of the ideas you could pursue, which one has characteristics that tilt toward power later? Why did you pick this one?
- Is your edge in AI built on something every competitor can also buy from the same model provider?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The deck lists "amazing team," "speed," or "execution" as the moat. These can be copied.
- The founder claims a flywheel or network effect but cannot show it is material to price or margin.
- The founder claims a data scale advantage when competitors have similar data and the curve has flattened.
- No counter positioning: an incumbent with the same capabilities can extend its product to cover the idea.
- Brand is listed as a startup advantage. Brand power and process power come only in the stability phase.
- The market is misdefined (assuming global scale benefits in a geographically local business).
- Endless strategy theorizing instead of action. Strategy should guide doing, not replace it.
