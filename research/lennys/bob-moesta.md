# The ultimate guide to JTBD — Bob Moesta

## Source

- **Speaker / author:** Bob Moesta (co-creator of Jobs to Be Done with Clay Christensen), interviewed by Lenny Rachitsky
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xQV7HVyAJjc
- **Type:** transcript

## Summary
Moesta argues that demand is not created by products. It is caused by a "struggling moment" in a specific context. People "hire" products to make progress. To find real demand you must study people who already switched, not people who say they want something. Switching happens only when the push of the current situation plus the pull of the new outcome beat the anxiety of the new plus the habit of the present. Context and outcome matter more than "pain and gain." For idea validation this is a direct test: can the founder name real people who recently changed behavior, what pushed them, and what they gave up?

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **People hire products to make progress:** Products are hired, not bought. The job is a context plus a desired outcome. Only people have jobs, not products or organizations.
- **Struggling moments cause demand:** Supply and demand are less connected than founders think. A struggling moment can exist for years with nobody solving it. Roadmaps should list struggling moments after the first 90 to 120 days, not features.
- **Context and outcome, not pain and gain:** The context makes a person value a solution in that moment. Together they form a "vector of progress." Value depends on where the person starts, not only where they end up. You can overshoot, and then people want a discount.
- **Four forces of progress:** F1 push of the situation, F2 pull of the new outcome, F3 anxiety of the new, F4 habit of the present. If F1 + F2 are not greater than F3 + F4, nobody moves. Without a push, people cannot even see your product. More features often add anxiety. Reducing friction can beat adding pull (condo deal with moving and two years of storage raised sales over 30%).
- **The demand-side competitive set:** Snickers competes with protein drinks, Red Bull and coffee (hunger, keep working). Milky Way competes with wine, a brownie, a run (emotional regroup). Look at the customer, not through your product, to see real competitors.
- **Timeline of a purchase (six phases):** First thought, passive looking (problem aware, solution unaware), active looking, deciding (trade-offs), first use, ongoing use (building the new habit). Autobooks split one demo into three, matched to the buyer's phase: sales cycle nearly halved, conversion up about 4x.
- **Interview only people who already made progress:** Talk to recent buyers or people who switched, including to a competitor's product. For a new product, ask "what will people stop using when your product comes out?" and interview those switchers (eBay, Etsy, Craigslist sellers for a marketplace).
- **Interview technique:** No fixed discussion guide. Use pushes, pulls, anxieties, habits as the frame. Say "tell me more" and "give me an example" instead of repeated "why." Play things back slightly wrong so people correct and elaborate. Bracket at the "edge of language" ("was it more this or that?"). Treat it as "criminal and intelligence interrogation that feels like therapy."
- **Layers of language:** Pablum layer ("it was good"), fantasy/nightmare layer (exaggeration), then what actually happened. The real story is often far longer than people remember (a coat rack took 18 months to buy).
- **Three kinds of energy:** Functional (time, space, effort, knowledge), emotional (how I feel), social (how others see me).
- **Sample size:** Patterns repeat at about 7 or 8 interviews. Do 10, no more than 12. Prefer two rounds of 12 over 24 at once.
- **Cluster, don't segment:** Find sets of pushes that go with sets of pulls. Most products are hired for 3 to 5 conflicting jobs (fast vs thorough). Intercom found four jobs, competed with HubSpot on one and Zendesk on another, and priced and packaged per job.
- **Talk to churned users:** When people leave, they are still making progress. Churn reveals the context that changed.
- **Choose what to suck at:** Most products fail because they made a trade-off the customer did not agree with. Your trade-offs must map the customer's trade-offs.
- **When JTBD does not work:** Where there is no real choice (employer health insurance) or deep habits (chewing gum, Tide) people cannot recall. Study use, or study switchers.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Struggling moment and context**
- Describe the exact struggling moment your product is for. Where is the person, what just happened, and what are they trying to get back to?
- What has to be true in someone's life for today to be "the day" they look for something new? What happened the day before?
- How long has this struggling moment existed, and why has nobody solved it yet?
- Where does your customer start from? If they start low, what is the smallest step up they would value? Are you overshooting?

**Evidence from real switchers**
- Name three people who recently switched to a solution like yours, or a competitor's. Walk me through their story from first thought to first use.
- What will people stop using when your product comes out? Have you interviewed people who recently switched to or away from those things?
- Have you talked to anyone who churned from a similar product? What changed in their context?
- Which of your "evidence" is people saying what they will do, versus people who already did something?

**Forces of progress**
- What is the push strong enough to break the habit of the present? Without it, can people even see your product?
- What anxieties will your product create? Will people worry it is too complex, too risky, or hard to switch to?
- What friction around adoption (data migration, learning, telling a boss) could you remove instead of adding features?
- What do people have to give up to use you, and who else do they have to convince?

**Competition and trade-offs**
- If you look from the customer's moment, not your product category, what are the real alternatives? Name a non-obvious one, like the "run" that competes with a Milky Way.
- What different jobs might people hire you for? Where do those jobs conflict, like fast vs thorough?
- What have you chosen to suck at? Would your target customer agree with that trade-off?
- Which of your planned features would make you better for power users but worse for the people who chose you because you were simple?

**Buying process**
- Where is a typical buyer in their timeline: passive looking, active looking, or deciding? How does your sales or onboarding change for each?
- What do functional, emotional, and social progress look like for your customer? Which one drives the decision?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The founder defined the jobs in a conference room and never interviewed people who switched.
- Evidence is complaints or stated intent ("I'd totally use this") rather than past switching behavior. "Bitchin' ain't switchin'."
- The pitch is "more features" or "better product" while the forces holding people in place (anxiety, habit) are ignored.
- The competitor list is just same-category products, seen from the supply side.
- Survey answers contradict behavior (93% said they wanted Energy Star; everyone bought the finished basement), and the founder trusts the survey.
- The product follows its best users upmarket and loses the simple core that made people hire it (the Basecamp Gantt chart trap).
- The product targets a choice people do not really make (no real choice) or a deep habit they cannot describe.
- The sales process pushes everyone to "demo then close" without asking where the buyer is in their timeline.
