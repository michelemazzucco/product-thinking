# Building product at Stripe — Jeff Weinstein

## Source
- **Title:** Building product at Stripe | Jeff Weinstein
- **Speaker / author:** Jeff Weinstein (product lead at Stripe for payment infrastructure, then Stripe Atlas; earlier a startup founder), interviewed by Lenny Rachitsky
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qbZQjprTnrU
- **Type:** transcript
- **Notes on source quality:** Clean, punctuated transcript. Very long episode (Lenny's longest at the time) with sponsor reads, a few "[inaudible]" marks and a couple of short repeated fragments where segments were stitched. Rich in concrete practices and his own startup failure story, which is directly about validating a problem.

## Summary
Weinstein's core lesson comes from his own failed startup. During a 20-minute outage, customers were not furious. That silence meant no product-market fit, and he missed it for years. Craft is "dessert" after the meal of solving a burning problem people badly need. He finds burning problems by listening in silence instead of pitching, by staying "text message friendly" with five to ten target customers, and by separating "ready to pay" from actually paying. He ignores feedback from friends. He picks metrics that measure customer value from the customer's view (Atlas: companies with zero support tickets, 15% to 85% in 18 months). For a founder, this source supplies hard tests: would your users riot if it went down, has anyone paid, and is your evidence from target customers or from friends?

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **The outage test:** At his SQL-analytics startup, a 10 to 20 minute outage drew only "little murmurs." Customers were not emailing, texting, or knocking on the door. That was the signal of no product-market fit. He spent years more on it before selling.
- **Craft is dessert:** No amount of craft, beauty or delight fixes a product people do not really need. First find problems "in which people will pause their entire day to solve." People "don't really get out of bed for their second problem."
- **Don't pitch; sit in silence:** Pitching anchors the customer on your guess and hides their top problem. Instead ask open prompts and wait: "Open up your email, what's in there?" "If you weren't talking to me, what would you be working on?" "What grinds your gears?" "Magic wand: what do you wish was off your plate?" "What did you do yesterday?" Your roadmap can emerge from what people say (Stripe Identity came from repeated complaints about bots and fraud).
- **Speed of response:** When a customer takes time to describe a problem, treat it as a "P-zero" gift. Reply fast, even to say "tomorrow." With detractors, aim to turn them into promoters (the Atlas "25 shares vs 25%" bug turned a critic into a long-term advisor).
- **Five to ten close customers:** Pick the customers who represent where the world is going (most ambitious, technical, fastest growing). Being "text message friendly" with five or ten of them gives more signal than large research.
- **Self-selection filters:** Ask for a screenshot, a video, or "three to five bullet points" by email. Those who follow through are worth going deep with. Bounded made-up programs (a "bug-finder program" run for three days) give people permission to engage. One produced 65 videos and dozens of issues.
- **Ignore friends; name the target customer:** Discount feedback from friends to zero. The target customer is not a segment but "Sarah in this department, who has these tabs open and just faced this problem and needs to solve it by 4:00 PM."
- **Ready to pay is not paying:** Test value with price: "this is $10,000, fully refundable" makes Sarah reveal what it would really need to solve. With enterprise or partner asks: "wire us $1 million, we'll wire it back anytime." Hesitation means you are about to build something that may never be used. He has founders practice by sending him a $1 invoice.
- **PMF = charts plus tweets:** The PM's job is to produce product-market fit. Evidence is quantitative (charts up and to the right) and qualitative (what people say, unprompted) side by side.
- **Metrics as customer value:** Measure the value you produce, from the customer's perspective. Not "logins" but "accomplished what they came for." A good metric is one the customer would be happy to see if your dashboard leaked. Use one overarching metric (companies with zero support tickets) plus tactical metrics owned by engineers. Prefer cohort charts that go "up and to the left" (time to outcome). Name metrics plainly so they motivate.
- **Users having a bad day:** Log an event any time a user likely had a bad day (404, late payout, many declines). Stack the reasons in a bar chart and burn them down. A new bad-day reason is product gold.
- **Go-to-market follows the job:** Founders choose an incorporation service by asking friends. So the strategy was to delight current customers so they recommend it. Metric, tactic and go-to-market line up.
- **Study Groups and friction logs:** Four to eight people from anywhere in the company pretend to be a made-up customer with a goal. Rule one: you do not work at Stripe (no internal knowledge). Rule two: do not solve problems, only practice empathy. Products rot with internal entropy; you need "unnatural" counterbalances.
- **Product includes the non-software parts:** Sales, support, onboarding calls, even a FedEx envelope (Fidelity) are product. A "leaky" self-serve funnel full of support contacts may be revealing what the product should be.
- **Go-go-go plus long-term compounding:** Move now with optimism, but also invest in what you will never regret (latency, reliability). For Stripe's global payment methods, rushing failed; slower platform building turned growth nonlinear.
- **Proof of existence:** For 83(b) automation, first mail one blank page through a vendor. "Look, we did it one time" beats debate. Storyboard the unconstrained ideal with a Sharpie.
- **Automate, don't compete on labor:** Atlas (about 10 people) only takes on work it can automate, aiming to "be the only" rather than "the best." It must be economically viable and show its economics.
- **Competitors are alternatives:** Staying open with AngelList led them to hand their incorporation business to Atlas. If you care about your customer, you care about their alternatives.
- **Problems hide in plain sight:** Atlas started from founders flying to the US just to form a US company, a burning problem. Weinstein chose another startup while the Collisons, with the same knowledge, chose online payments.
- **Get stuck on problems one and two:** John Collison told him he was great at problems three to 100 but needed to be stuck on one and two.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Burning problem**
- If your product went down for 20 minutes tomorrow, who would email, text, or call you? How many? How angry?
- Is this your customer's first problem, or their second or fifth? How do you know?
- Describe your target customer at the "Sarah" level: role, the tabs she has open, what she must solve by 4 PM today.
- When you talk to prospects, do you pitch first? What did they bring up before you mentioned your product?
- Ask a prospect "what's in your inbox right now?" and "what did you do yesterday?" Did your problem come up without prompting?
- Would someone "leap through the computer" when they hear what you do? Who has done that so far?

**Evidence quality**
- How much of your positive feedback comes from friends, ex-colleagues, or people who like you? What is left if you count it as zero?
- Who are the five to ten target customers you are text-message friendly with today? When did you last talk to each?
- When someone took time to describe a problem, how fast did you reply?

**Paying, not willing**
- Has anyone paid you yet, even $1? If not, what stops you from asking today?
- If you told a prospect "it's $10,000, fully refundable," what would they say it must do? Have you asked?
- For a big customer or partner asking for custom features: would they wire money upfront? What happens if they say no?

**Measuring value**
- What is the one metric that measures value from your customer's perspective? Would they be happy if your dashboard leaked?
- What does a customer "having a bad day" with your product look like? List five events you would log.
- How will you know you have product-market fit: which chart, and which unprompted quotes?

**Strategy and scope**
- How do your customers find solutions like yours (friends, search, an advisor)? Does your growth plan match that?
- What is the smallest "proof of existence" you can do this week to show the hardest part is possible?
- Which parts of the job could you automate to be the only option, rather than compete on labor to be the best?
- Is this business economically viable at scale? How do you charge people who may not have money yet?
- Who are the alternatives your customers use today, and could you partner with them instead of fighting?
- What are your problems one and two right now? Are you staying busy on three through 100 instead?

## Red flags / failure modes
- Users shrug when the product breaks. No anger means no burning need.
- Polishing craft and delight on a product nobody badly needs.
- Founder pitches and demos in customer calls instead of listening, so the real top problem never surfaces.
- Evidence comes mostly from friends and friendly beta users who "like it" and click around.
- Confusing "they said they'd pay" or "they looked at the contract" with actual payment.
- Building a long feature list for one big enterprise or partner promise with no upfront commitment ("the siren song of the big three-year contract").
- Metrics that count internal events (logins, signups) instead of customer outcomes, or metrics nobody looks at regularly.
- A target customer described as a segment ("digital natives") rather than a specific person with a specific moment.
- Staying busy on many small problems while avoiding the one or two hardest ones.
