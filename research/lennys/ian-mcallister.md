## Summary
McAllister covers two topics: the skills of a top 1% PM, and Amazon's "working backwards" process. Working backwards means starting from a customer problem and staying faithful to it, then designing the solution. The internal press release and FAQ (PR/FAQ) are only a mechanism to enforce this. The most common failure is having a solution first and retrofitting a problem to it ("ingredients in the pantry"). Bezos's three tests for a new investment: is it a big idea, should we be the ones doing it, and is there a legitimate plan to succeed. The FAQ is where that plan is tested. For a founder, this is a ready-made check: can you write the problem paragraph before the solution, and does the problem feel compelling before anyone hears the solution?

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Working backwards = start from the problem:** Obsess over the customer's problem, then go to the solution. If you say "we could build X" without a customer problem, you are not working backwards, even if you use the template.
- **The PR/FAQ is a mechanism, not the point:** The internal press release has a problem paragraph, then a solution paragraph, then a customer quote. The FAQ asks whether there is a legitimate plan to succeed (internal components, finances, key technical hurdles). Once a team has the muscle, any format works.
- **"No problem paragraph, no problem":** McAllister once brought Bezos a press release without a problem paragraph. Bezos said maybe that meant there was no real problem. McAllister had the solution in mind first.
- **The "ingredients in the pantry" warning sign:** "We have these two technologies or assets, let's combine them" is the classic non-working-backwards pitch. His early "ASIN to ASIN linking" project was built from a Bezos sketch and team excitement, not a customer problem. It failed and he shut it down.
- **The compelling-problem test:** A good pitch has a customer problem that feels compelling even before you know the solution. A big audience and a painful problem (painkiller, not vitamin), then a novel way to solve it.
- **Bezos's three investment criteria:** Is it a big idea? Is it something we should be doing (shale oil extraction may be big, but not for Amazon)? Is there a legitimate plan to succeed? You need all three.
- **Efficiency comes later:** Bezos: an exploration process does not need to be efficient, because efficiency requires knowing where you are going.
- **Working backwards review:** A meeting where leaders review the press release and FAQ. It can be the gate to approval. AmazonSmile was greenlit this way after several concepts were reviewed over months.
- **Fitness function:** Pick one metric that defines success for a business and drive it "up and to the right," not just to hit a goal. Prioritize the projects with the greatest leverage on it.
- **Think big:** Before starting, ask whether the idea could be bigger and more impactful. Start small if you must, but with the bigger vision in mind ("hunt for bigger elephants").
- **Forecast, execute, measure:** What separates PMs from consultants is that they forecast, ship, then measure and validate. This builds instincts.
- **Trust is the currency:** Built by repeatedly setting and meeting expectations. Call your shots, launch what and when you said, own mistakes. A right strategy fails if the people who must carry it out are not truly aligned (his Airbnb support lesson).
- **Prioritization is the core skill:** Themes, projects, sequencing, how much of a project to build, and your own time. A good prioritizer can generate 5x the impact.
- **Operate what you built:** Weekly business reviews force you to know your metrics and explain variances. Watching the product you run gives ideas for what to double down on or prevent.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Problem first**
- Write the problem paragraph of your press release now, without mentioning your solution. Does it stand on its own?
- If I removed every mention of your product, would a stranger still find this problem compelling?
- Which came first: the customer problem, or a technology or asset you wanted to use? Be honest.
- Are you combining "ingredients in the pantry" (a model, an API, a dataset you have) and then looking for someone to feed?
- Who is the specific customer in the press release, and what quote would they give in their own words after using it?

**Big idea, right owner, legitimate plan**
- Is this a big idea? If it works perfectly, how large can it get?
- Why are you the right person or company to do this? What makes it something you, specifically, should be doing?
- What is your legitimate plan to succeed? List the hardest technical hurdle, the finances, and the dependencies you do not control.
- What questions would a skeptical reviewer put in your FAQ? Which one can you not answer yet?
- Could this be bigger than you are pitching it? What would the bigger version look like, and what would you start with?

**Measurement and forecasting**
- What single metric defines success for this product? What number do you forecast for it in 6 and 12 months?
- How will you know you were wrong? What result would make you stop?
- After launch, what will you review every week to understand what is really happening?

**People and trust**
- Whose support do you need to make this work (partners, early customers, a sales channel)? Are they truly aligned, or just nodding?
- What expectation have you set with investors or early users, and have you met it so far?

## Red flags / failure modes
- The pitch starts with technology or available assets, not a customer problem ("we could combine these two things").
- The founder cannot write a convincing problem paragraph, or writes it after the solution.
- Team excitement or a boss's sketch drives the project, not evidence of a customer problem (the ASIN-to-ASIN trap).
- Using the PR/FAQ template as theater while the solution was decided in advance.
- A big idea with no legitimate plan to succeed: hard technical, financial or operational hurdles not thought through.
- A good idea that is not something this founder or company should be doing.
- No metric, no forecast, so nothing can be validated after launch.
- Assuming key stakeholders are aligned without the work to earn their trust and support.
