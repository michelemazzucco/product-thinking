# Inside Linear: Building with taste, craft, and focus — Karri Saarinen

## Source
- **Title:** Inside Linear: Building with taste, craft, and focus | Karri Saarinen
- **Speaker / author:** Karri Saarinen (co-founder and CEO of Linear; formerly founding designer at Coinbase, principal designer at Airbnb), interviewed by Lenny Rachitsky
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4muxFVZ4XfM
- **Type:** transcript
- **Notes on source quality:** Clean, punctuated transcript with sponsor reads and a few "[inaudible]" marks. One small stitching glitch where a sentence repeats mid-file. ## Summary
Saarinen explains how Linear builds with taste and craft instead of A/B tests and feature metrics. Craft does not mean slow perfection: features ship to the team in week one, then to opt-in customers, and only get polished before general release. The "science" is constant customer contact by the whole team; the "magic" is judgment built on that understanding. In crowded markets, design raises the bar to even be considered, but design is never the reason a company succeeds on its own. Product-market fit is a spectrum, measured per segment. Linear spent two years becoming the default tool for early-stage startups before working on larger companies. For idea validation, this source asks: who exactly is the first segment, is the product opinionated for them, are you in a retention or transactional business, and are you working on the main quest?

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **The problem came from lived experience:** The three founders worked at several companies and saw that project tools felt clunky, complex, and built on a legacy way of thinking. They set out to build the most frictionless system for modern software development.
- **Craft is not perfectionism:** Put the feature into the app in the first week, visible only to the team. Then open it to opt-in customers, janky is fine. Polish before general release. Building reveals how it should work better than upfront design.
- **Co-build with one to ten customers:** For large-company features, find a real company (like Vercel on roadmaps) to explain how they work and give feedback along the way. It is hard to imagine these workflows alone.
- **Design as differentiator:** "The more it matters, the more the design matters." In new paradigms, first products can be rough. In crowded categories (like email clients), the design bar is high just to be considered. Design is leverage early, when nobody knows you, like Apple's packaging setting expectations. But "design is never going to be the reason why a company is successful." The product still has to be better or different.
- **Brand is built, not declared:** Brand is not logo and colors. It is built over time by what you do and say, how you treat customers, how you build. A strong brand makes people stop thinking about alternatives (Airbnb as a direct destination).
- **Opinionated software:** Flexible software makes every team figure out its own way. Linear gives good defaults so users focus on work, not the tool. "Design something for someone"; designing for everyone yields a generic solution.
- **No feature metrics, no A/B tests:** There may be a company-level goal (like weekly active users), but no metric targets per feature. Success is customers agreeing the problem is solved. Data is used to answer specific questions, not to make decisions. People often lean on data from fear of being wrong.
- **Magic and science:** Science = everyone talks to users: weekly customer calls, shared Slack channels with customers, recorded calls and notes. Magic = intuition grounded in that understanding.
- **Retention and trust business:** Linear wants companies to adopt early and stay forever, so it must keep delivering quality and not mistreat them. Transactional businesses can choose differently. The way you build should fit your domain.
- **Focus: main quest vs side quests:** YC advice: talk to customers, build the product, exercise. Ask "is this important now, or later?" Example: SOC 2 was needed eventually, so they told customers "not yet." Most people were fine with it.
- **Waitlist with a survey:** The launch announcement was written in the founders' own voice, aimed at the right audience. The waitlist (about 4,000 in a month) asked what tools people used, company size, and why they wanted Linear. Karri hand-picked invites, about 10 people a week at first, emailed personally, and followed up after a few days.
- **Cohort onboarding:** Invite a small cohort, fix the problems they hit, then invite the next. Inviting everyone at once means everyone hits the same bug.
- **Pay-what-you-want slider:** During beta, an optional settings page let teams choose a per-seat price (some paid $1, some the $20 maximum). It gave confidence a market existed under $20. At public launch after about a year, nearly all beta companies converted to paid.
- **PMF is a spectrum, by segment:** Always ask "with whom do we have fit, and how strong?" Linear first aimed to be the default tool for startups. A Fortune 500 company was not a fit then. Double down where something works (Zoom kept going after more universities). Watch for emerging pull (crypto, then AI companies).
- **Go slow to go fast:** Too much urgency means rushing, then going back to fix.

## Strong quotes
> "the more it matters, the more the design matters."

> "design is never going to be the reason why a company is successful. It's like the company still has to have some other things. The product still needs to be something, it needs to be better in some ways or it needs to be different in some ways."

> "my design mantra is always design something for someone. It is very hard to design everything for everyone because you just end up with a very generalized solution."

> "ideally, the success what it looks like customers agree that the problem is solved or they enjoy the solution and it's not like that the metrics went up."

> "Well, does it help the customers? Does it help the product?" This sounds like a side quest to me, and basically means we shouldn't do it.

> "Do we have the fit in the specific segments?" And how strong that fit is.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Segment and fit**
- Which single segment do you want to become the default tool for first? Why them, and who is explicitly not a fit yet?
- If a big enterprise customer asked to use it tomorrow, could you serve them? If not, are you comfortable saying no?
- How will you know your fit with the first segment is strong, not just present? What would make you double down on one type of customer?
- Where is pull already showing up (a type of company, an industry) that you are not yet following?

**Why you, why now, why better**
- Have you lived this problem yourself at several companies? What exactly felt broken?
- Your product looks great. Beyond design, how is it better or different in a way a user would notice without seeing a screenshot?
- How crowded is this category? Is a high design bar the price of entry, or the actual advantage?

**Opinionated product**
- What is the one opinionated way of working your product enforces? Who will disagree with it, and are you fine losing them?
- Who is the "someone" you are designing for? Describe one real person, not a persona.
- Where are you adding flexibility because you have not decided what the right default is?

**Learning loop**
- How soon can a real customer touch a rough version? What would stop you shipping it to yourselves in week one?
- Which one to ten customers will co-build the first version with you and explain how they actually work?
- How will you onboard early users in small cohorts so you fix one round of problems before the next?
- How will you define success for a feature if not a metric? What would a customer say when the problem is solved?

**Business model and focus**
- Are you in a retention business or a transactional one? How does that change how much you invest in quality and trust?
- Before launch, what is your evidence people will pay, and roughly how much? Could you test it with an optional payment page?
- List what you plan to do in the next three months. Which items are side quests that do not move the main quest?
- What can you tell early customers "not yet" (compliance, integrations, enterprise features) without losing them?

**Launch**
- Who is your first audience, where are they, and what language do they use? Is your announcement written in your own voice for them?

## Red flags / failure modes
- Treating design as the whole strategy. A beautiful product that is not better or different still fails.
- Trying to serve everyone from day one instead of winning one segment first.
- Flexible, configurable product because the founder has not formed an opinion on the right workflow.
- Perfectionism that blocks shipping. No real user touches anything until it is "done."
- Letting data make decisions out of fear, with no direct customer contact behind it.
- Calling PMF a yes/no answer, without saying with whom and how strong.
- Opening the doors to the whole waitlist at once, so all users hit the same early problems.
- Spending time on side quests (merch, certifications, events, podcasts) before the core product works for core customers.
- A corporate-sounding launch that copies big companies and does not speak to the real first audience.
