## Summary
Doshi covers five ideas: pre-mortems, the LNO framework for time, the three levels of product work (impact, execution, optics), why most execution problems are really strategy, culture or interpersonal problems, and prioritizing by opportunity cost instead of ROI. A bonus idea is high agency. For validating an idea, two tools stand out. The pre-mortem ("imagine this has failed six months from now; what went wrong?") surfaces the risks a founder is avoiding. The opportunity-cost lens asks whether this idea is the best use of the founder's time, not merely a positive one. He also offers a sharp test from Jack Dorsey: how does this make users love the product more?

## Key ideas and frameworks
- **Pre-mortem:** Before a launch or project, the leader gives the prompt: imagine this has failed miserably six months from now. Work backwards: what caused it? The hypothetical failure creates psychological safety to raise concerns people would otherwise hide to stay positive.
- **Tigers, paper tigers, elephants:** Each person brings three kinds of items. A tiger is a real threat that will kill the project. A paper tiger is a threat others worry about but you do not. An elephant is the thing nobody is talking about (example: "we assume PR will bring users, but are we sure?"). The words become shared vocabulary.
- **How to run it:** Invite every function involved; for big launches, split into a product/engineering session and a go-to-market session. Alternate quiet writing time and sharing. Each person votes for the scariest tiger someone else raised. The leader prioritizes and publishes a post-pre-mortem action plan, and owns it. The point is not to solve everything but to find unspoken or unowned threats.
- **LNO framework:** Leverage tasks return 10x to 100x the effort. Neutral tasks return about what you put in. Overhead tasks return less. Be a perfectionist only on L tasks; do N and O tasks quickly. The same activity (a bug report, meeting notes) can be L, N or O depending on context.
- **You already know your L tasks:** They are the ones you procrastinate on, like strategy, because they are hard and you fear having nothing good to say. "Not enough time" is the excuse. Tactics: a couple of days of "placebo productivity" on N and O tasks, then change location to do the L task.
- **Three levels of product work:** Impact (what difference it makes), execution (how to get it done), optics (creating awareness of the impact and execution). Conflicts between smart people often come from operating at different levels, like a PM at execution and a CEO at impact. Early-stage teams, once they have a reasonable hypothesis, should mostly optimize for execution, since impact will not show in weeks.
- **Optics can become the goal:** Internal optics are useful (energy, feedback). But if a culture rewards status updates over results, optics replaces impact.
- **Most execution problems are not execution problems:** In high-performing teams, they are usually strategy problems (no clear strategy, so teams misalign), culture problems (incentives like OKRs that punish helping), or interpersonal problems. Real execution problems exist (old infrastructure, skills gaps). Test: if the band-aid keeps falling off, it was not an execution problem.
- **Minimize opportunity cost, not maximize ROI:** In high-leverage roles there are hundreds of positive-ROI things. ROI thinking shrinks the denominator (time), so teams fill up on quick wins. Opportunity cost asks "is this the best use of my time?" not "is this a good use?" Do not try to quantify it; give explicit permission instead (for example 60% incrementals, 30% big new initiatives, 10% stability). At Stripe Connect, this shift led to a big, scary bet driven by a customer need he expected to grow.
- **Think big:** At Google, a common rule was that ideas under $100M were not worth discussing. Ask: there is a $1M business here, can it be $10M? The downside: Google missed some trends because of this high bar.
- **Differentiation plus network effects:** Twitter's staying power came from combining core product differentiation (still no product like it) with network effects.
- **Talk in customer terms:** From John Collison: separate the effort spent building a feature from the effort spent talking about it. The things that resonate with customers may not be what took the most work.
- **High agency:** Getting what you want without waiting for perfect conditions or blaming circumstances. Three parts: ownership, creative execution, resilience.

## Questions this source makes you ask about an idea
**Pre-mortem**
- Imagine it is six months after launch and this product has failed miserably. Write the story of what went wrong. What is the first cause you wrote?
- What is your tiger: the one threat that could actually kill this? What are you doing about it this week?
- What is your elephant: the uncomfortable assumption nobody, including you, is saying out loud? (For example: "we assume launch buzz will bring users.")
- Which risks are paper tigers that others will raise but you can show do not matter? How do you know?
- Which risk are you assuming someone else will handle, when in fact nobody owns it?
- Split your risks: what kills the product side, and what kills the go-to-market side?

**Opportunity cost**
- Is this idea the best use of your next year, or just a positive one? What is the strongest alternative you are giving up?
- Are you picking this idea because it is a sure, small win, while a more ambiguous, bigger idea scares you?
- There is a small business here. What would it take to make it 10x bigger? Is that path real?

**Impact vs execution vs optics**
- How exactly does this make users love the product, or their work, more? Answer without metrics or features.
- What is your reasonable hypothesis about what will win? Is it strong enough that you can now focus on execution?
- Are you spending time on pitch decks, launch buzz and announcements (optics) before you have shown impact?

**Strategy and differentiation**
- What is your core product differentiation, the thing that means "there is still no product like it"? Is there any network effect on top of it?
- Which features will you talk about to customers, and are they the ones customers care about or the ones that were hardest to build?
- What is the L task for this idea that you keep postponing (talking to customers, writing the strategy, pricing)? Why?
- When things go wrong, are you waiting for better conditions (funding, a co-founder, a better tool)? What would a high-agency version of you do now?

## Red flags / failure modes
- No pre-mortem: the founder has never written down how the idea fails, so obvious problems show up as public backlash after launch.
- An unspoken elephant, such as assuming launch PR alone will bring users.
- A plan full of quick, positive-ROI tasks while the big, ambiguous bet is never examined.
- Procrastinating on the hard "L task" (strategy, customer conversations) and blaming lack of time.
- Optics replacing impact: status updates, announcements and buzz celebrated over real results.
- Recurring "execution" problems that keep returning after band-aids, pointing to an unclear strategy underneath.
- The founder cannot answer how the product makes users love it more, only how it moves metrics.
- Marketing the features that took the most effort, not the ones customers understand and value.
